What's Included in AC Repair Service

A standard visit starts with a full diagnostic inspection — the technician checks refrigerant levels, electrical components, airflow, and the condition of the indoor and outdoor units before recommending a fix. Common repairs performed on the spot include:

  • Capacitor and contactor replacement
  • Refrigerant leak detection and recharge
  • Condenser coil cleaning
  • Blower motor and fan motor repair
  • Thermostat diagnostics and replacement
  • Condensate drain line clearing
  • Ductwork leak inspection

A written quote is provided before any repair work begins. Full system replacement is handled as a separate estimate, not bundled into a repair visit.

Common Signs Your AC Needs Repair

These are the warning signs that typically mean a system needs a professional look, not a DIY fix:

  • Warm or lukewarm air coming from the vents
  • The system runs constantly without reaching the thermostat's set temperature
  • Ice forming on the refrigerant lines or the indoor coil
  • Grinding, clicking, or loud humming with little or no airflow
  • Water pooling near the indoor unit
  • A sudden spike in the electric bill with no change in usage
  • Weak airflow from some or all vents
  • The breaker trips repeatedly when the system starts
  • A burning or musty odor when the system runs

What usually causes these problems

Most no-cool and weak-cooling calls trace back to one of a short list of causes: a refrigerant leak, a failed run or start capacitor, a dirty or failed condenser fan motor, a clogged air filter restricting airflow, a frozen evaporator coil (usually itself caused by low airflow or low refrigerant), a wiring or electrical control failure, or — on older systems — a failing compressor. A clogged condensate drain line is the most common cause of water pooling near an indoor unit. A technician's diagnostic visit identifies which of these is actually happening in your system rather than guessing from symptoms alone.

Repair or Replace? A Straightforward Way to Decide

Age and repair cost are the two things that actually matter here. ENERGY STAR notes that systems over 10–12 years old are "more prone to breakdowns and performance issues" and less efficient than current equipment[1] — under that age, a repair is almost always the better value. Past that age, especially if the system still runs on R-22 refrigerant, replacement more often makes financial sense: U.S. production and import of R-22 ended January 1, 2020 under EPA's Section 608 phaseout program, so remaining supply is limited to recovered and reclaimed refrigerant only[2] — a leak repair on an R-22 system can cost meaningfully more than the same repair on a newer refrigerant.

A common industry rule of thumb for the middle ground: multiply the unit's age in years by the estimated repair cost in dollars — if that number comes out above roughly $5,000, replacement is usually the better value than another repair. It's a rule of thumb, not a guarantee, but it's a reasonable gut check before committing to a major repair on an older system.
