## 1. Your Role and Mission

Act as a senior software architect, AI systems engineer, research engineer, scientific fact-checker, editorial director, SEO content strategist, and quality-assurance engineer.

Your task is to audit, repair, and substantially improve my existing automatic article-generation and publishing application.

The application already exists and uses an OpenRouter API key to generate content. Its exact architecture, programming language, framework, data storage, publishing integrations, and workflow implementation must be discovered by inspecting the existing project. Do not assume these details.

### Current problems

The current automation has repeatedly produced unacceptable results despite more than two weeks of attempted fixes:

- Grammarly reports very high AI-writing scores, sometimes exceeding 87%.
- Articles have plagiarism and originality concerns.
- The writing is generic, repetitive, artificial, and often poor quality.
- Academic references may be invented, mismatched, or irrelevant.
- Scientific and technical claims may be unsupported or internally inconsistent.
- Articles repeat information across sections and contain filler.
- Internal links are not reliably selected for the correct website.
- Multiple websites must be managed independently without cross-site contamination.
- The system does not reliably establish whether the content is factually correct before publication.

I do not want another round of superficial prompt tweaks. I want a technically sound, maintainable, evidence-driven content production system.

### Primary objective

Build an automation that consistently produces original, well-researched, genuinely useful, publication-quality articles for multiple websites, with a reliable mechanism to identify and stop bad content before it is published.

Optimize for quality, factual integrity, originality, editorial excellence, maintainability, and successful publication—not maximum output volume or minimum API cost.

Target volume: approximately 30 articles per month across all configured websites.

## 2. Non-Negotiable Engineering Rules

1. Inspect the existing repository, application entry points, dependencies, configuration, database, prompts, model calls, publishing logic, and error handling before modifying anything.

2. Preserve working functionality wherever possible. Do not rewrite the entire application merely because a different architecture looks attractive.

3. Identify the actual causes of failure and produce a short technical audit before implementing changes.

4. Use OpenRouter for model access. Do not assume that the current model is an OpenAI model or that any particular provider is already configured.

5. Do not hard-code unverified model IDs, pricing, context limits, or capabilities.

6. Do not invent source documents, research findings, experimental measurements, quotations, authors, references, URLs, internal links, or credentials.

7. Never use an AI detector score as proof of authorship or as the sole criterion for content acceptance. Improve genuine editorial quality rather than using paraphrasing tricks or detector-evasion techniques.

8. Never publish an article simply because generation completed successfully.

9. Keep secrets out of prompts, article content, logs, generated reports, source-control commits, and user-visible output.

10. Make the implementation testable. Add automated tests, failure scenarios, structured logs, configuration validation, and clear documentation.

11. Do not claim that a feature works until it has been implemented and tested.

12. If a required service, API key, database, or integration is unavailable, implement the appropriate interface or configuration and clearly identify what remains unverified. Never fabricate a successful test.

## 6. Research Before Writing

A major requirement is to prevent the model from inventing research after it has already written the article.

### Research workflow

1. Parse the topic, intended audience, target country, search intent, article type, and requested level of evidence.
2. Retrieve the correct site's existing content inventory.
3. Identify relevant primary sources, official documentation, original research, recognized professional guidance, and other suitable authoritative material.
4. Retrieve the actual source content through supported research tools, APIs, or accessible documents.
5. Store source metadata and the relevant supporting passages.
6. Identify conflicting findings, missing evidence, publication dates, limitations, and potentially outdated guidance.
7. Construct a research brief and evidence pack.
8. Draft only after the research pack has passed basic validation.

A search-result snippet alone is insufficient evidence for a detailed scientific, technical, medical, legal, or other high-impact claim.

If a source cannot be accessed, mark it as inaccessible. Do not pretend to have read it.

### Research source hierarchy

Select sources according to the subject, including:

- Original research and primary datasets.
- Official government and regulatory publications.
- Official technical documentation.
- Systematic reviews and high-quality scholarly reviews.
- Professional standards and recognized institutional guidance.
- Reputable secondary sources for context where appropriate.

Do not apply a rigid hierarchy without considering the question. A current official regulation may be more relevant than an older paper, while a primary experiment may be necessary to verify a particular experimental result.

### Source record

Each source record should include, when available:

- Stable source ID.
- Title.
- Author or issuing organization.
- Publication date and retrieval date.
- Canonical URL.
- DOI, PMID, or other bibliographic identifier.
- Source type.
- Relevant quoted or extracted passage.
- Access and verification status.
- Claims supported by the source.
- Limitations and potential conflicts of interest.

Keep source records separate from generated prose.

## 7. Claim-Level Evidence and Hallucination Controls

Implement a claim-and-evidence ledger for every article that requires factual verification.

Each material factual claim must be represented in a structured record containing:

- Claim ID.
- Claim text.
- Claim category.
- Supporting source IDs.
- Exact supporting passages or evidence locations.
- Verification status.
- Confidence or uncertainty notes.
- Known limitations or conflicting evidence.
- Reviewer or validation history where applicable.

### Required claim statuses

Use explicit statuses such as:

- Supported.
- Partially supported.
- Contradicted.
- Unsupported.
- Unverified.
- Not applicable to factual verification.

Do not allow the model to label its own claim as verified merely because it generated a citation.

The verification process must independently compare the claim with the retrieved evidence.

### Mandatory rules

- A reference must support the specific claim to which it is attached.
- A real source that does not support the claim is a verification failure.
- A plausible-sounding citation is not evidence.
- A fabricated number must not be converted into a factual statement.
- Conflicting source results must be disclosed, resolved with adequate evidence, or escalated.
- Distinguish established findings from hypotheses, interpretations, and recommendations.
- Never invent experimental data or present simulated values as measured results.
- If a fact cannot be verified, omit it, qualify it appropriately, or hold the article.
- Preserve material uncertainty and limitations.
- Do not invent certainty to make the article sound authoritative.

For empirical articles, every reported measurement must have traceable provenance. If original experimental records are not supplied, the article must not claim that the measurements were personally observed or experimentally confirmed.

## 8. Academic Reference Verification

This stage is mandatory for articles that cite academic literature.

Implement bibliographic verification using suitable official or recognized metadata services, including:

- Crossref REST API: https://api.crossref.org/
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/
- Europe PMC, where appropriate.
- DOI resolution and publisher pages where available.

### Verification procedure

1. Extract every citation from the article.
2. Resolve its DOI, PMID, or other supplied identifier.
3. Retrieve the bibliographic record.
4. Compare title, authors, journal, publication year, DOI, and identifier.
5. Confirm that the source exists and is not merely a plausible reference.
6. Retrieve and inspect the original paper, abstract, full text, or accessible authoritative record as appropriate.
7. Check whether the cited source actually supports the associated claim.
8. Flag retracted publications, corrections, expressions of concern, and significant limitations when detectable.
9. Preserve a verification report for each reference.

Do not invent missing bibliographic details.

An unresolved source must remain unverified. It must not be silently accepted.

If a source is behind a paywall, do not claim to have checked inaccessible full text. State what could be verified from the accessible metadata or abstract and what remains uncertain.

## 9. Article Generation and Exceptional Editorial Quality

The writing model must receive a structured evidence pack, an approved outline, the site's editorial configuration, and the intended audience.

Do not rely on a generic instruction such as "write a comprehensive, human-sounding, SEO-optimized article."

### Article planning

Before drafting, determine:

- The specific question the article must answer.
- The reader's knowledge level and likely needs.
- Search intent and expected information.
- What the article can contribute that existing pages do not.
- The necessary concepts, evidence, examples, and caveats.
- What should be excluded because it is unsupported, redundant, or irrelevant.
- The correct article type and appropriate structure.

### Writing requirements

Articles must:

- Answer the reader's actual question early.
- Explain concepts accurately and in a logical sequence.
- Use concrete, topic-specific explanations rather than generic filler.
- Include meaningful examples, comparisons, or implications when supported.
- Preserve scientific and technical nuance.
- Distinguish facts from interpretation.
- Avoid repetitive introductions, summaries, and conclusions.
- Avoid repeating the same information across headings.
- Avoid fabricated quotations, anecdotes, case studies, or first-person experiences.
- Avoid meaningless keyword repetition.
- Avoid excessive headings, lists, tables, and formulaic "key takeaway" sections.
- Use headings that accurately describe their contents.
- Include citations close to the claims they support.
- Maintain consistent terminology and numerical values throughout the document.
- Avoid claiming expertise, research experience, or experimental work that the author did not perform.

Do not impose arbitrary word counts. Article length must be justified by the topic, reader intent, evidence, and competing information needs.

### Natural editorial quality

The goal is genuinely good writing, not evasion of AI detectors.

Require the system to remove:

- Generic opening paragraphs that add no value.
- Repetitive transitions.
- Inflated or unnatural phrasing.
- Empty claims about the importance of a topic.
- Redundant conclusions.
- Unnecessary restatement of headings.
- Unsubstantiated superlatives.
- Formulaic advice that could apply to almost any article.
- Repeated sentences that differ only slightly in wording.

Vary sentence structure where it improves readability, not merely to manipulate detection software.

Do not use random errors, awkward prose, artificial personal anecdotes, deliberate misspellings, or synonym substitution as supposed solutions to AI detection.

### Whole-document editing

After all sections are assembled, perform a dedicated editorial pass across the entire article.

Check for:

- Duplicate explanations.
- Contradictory claims.
- Inconsistent terminology.
- Conflicting figures, dates, units, and methods.
- Broken logical progression.
- Unnecessary sections.
- Abrupt transitions.
- Missing explanations.
- Unjustified conclusions.
- Inconsistent references.
- Repeated or irrelevant internal links.

A section that passes individually must still be reviewed in the context of the complete document.

## 10. Plagiarism and Originality Checks

Implement multiple complementary checks rather than relying on a single language model.

### Required checks

A. Compare the draft with its supplied source documents.

B. Compare it against the relevant site's existing articles.

C. Compare it against other articles produced or stored by the application, including other configured sites where authorized.

D. Detect unusually similar passages, copied sentence sequences, repeated section structures, and near-duplicate paragraphs.

E. Identify missing attribution for quotations and distinctive claims.

F. Detect content that has merely rearranged or lightly paraphrased a source without contributing adequate original analysis.

Where appropriate, integrate a dedicated text-similarity or plagiarism-detection service. Evaluate the service's coverage, licensing, privacy policy, API availability, and limitations before implementation.

### Similarity reporting

The report must distinguish:

- Exact textual overlap.
- Near-verbatim overlap.
- Common terminology and standard expressions.
- Properly attributed quotations.
- Bibliographic references.
- Template or boilerplate text.
- Similarity with the site's own earlier content.
- Potentially duplicated content across different websites.

Do not treat a raw similarity percentage as definitive proof of plagiarism.

There is no universal similarity threshold that makes every article safe. Calibrate thresholds against reviewed examples and use severity-based decisions.

When suspicious overlap is found, return the relevant passage, matching source, similarity evidence, and recommended action.

Do not automatically rewrite a suspicious passage merely to hide the similarity. Establish whether it is properly attributed, technically necessary, inaccurate, or genuinely derivative, then correct it appropriately.

## 11. Site-Specific Knowledge and Isolation

The application must support multiple websites without mixing their content, URLs, editorial settings, media, or publication identities.

Every article job must explicitly identify its target site.

### Per-site configuration

Store and validate:

- Site ID and canonical domain.
- Brand identity and editorial voice.
- Target geography and audience.
- Subject-matter scope.
- Content policies and prohibited claims.
- Preferred and disallowed terminology.
- SEO conventions.
- Existing page inventory and sitemap.
- Internal-link inventory.
- Categories and tags.
- Author and attribution rules.
- Image and media rules.
- Schema and metadata requirements.
- Publishing destination and authorization.
- Required approval policy.
- Applicable legal or regulatory constraints.

### Isolation requirements

- Never use another site's URLs unless an explicitly authorized external reference is intended.
- Never insert another site's internal links as if they belonged to the current site.
- Never reuse another site's author identity, logo, image assets, or publishing credentials by accident.
- Never assume that similar niches imply interchangeable content.
- Never expose private configuration from one site in another site's job logs or generated articles.
- Use site-scoped database queries and authorization checks.
- Validate the target site again immediately before publication.

Treat site isolation as an enforceable software rule, not a writing instruction.

## 12. Internal-Link Intelligence

Internal links must be selected from verified site data.

Create or maintain a crawlable page inventory with:

- Canonical URL.
- Page title.
- Main topic and related entities.
- Page type.
- Relevant target query or intent, where available.
- HTTP status.
- Canonical status.
- Indexability where detectable.
- Last verification time.
- Existing link relationships.

### Link selection

1. Identify concepts in the article that deserve an internal link.
2. Retrieve relevant destination candidates from the correct site's inventory.
3. Rank candidates by topical relevance, reader value, page purpose, and contextual fit.
4. Select a link only if it improves navigation or explanation.
5. Generate descriptive anchor text that accurately represents the destination.
6. Validate the destination and domain before accepting the link.
7. Record the source page, destination page, anchor text, and validation result.

Do not invent URLs. Do not force an arbitrary number of links into every article.

Do not link every keyword, repeat the same anchor excessively, or use unrelated money pages simply to improve an SEO score.

If no relevant internal page exists, leave the concept unlinked and record the opportunity. Never fabricate a destination to satisfy a link quota.

External citations and internal links must be represented as separate link types.

## 14. Automated Quality Gates

Implement deterministic validation wherever possible, supplemented by independent model review and human review when necessary.

### Gate A — Source integrity

Check that required sources resolve and that their metadata matches the cited references.

### Gate B — Evidence integrity

Check that material claims have traceable support and that unsupported or contradicted claims are resolved or removed.

### Gate C — Originality

Run configured similarity checks and flag severe or unexplained overlap.

### Gate D — Editorial quality

Evaluate relevance, clarity, structure, completeness, repetition, specificity, and evidence use.

### Gate E — Internal links

Validate domain ownership, destination availability, canonicalization, topical relevance, and anchor text.

### Gate F — Site integrity

Confirm that the article, media, author, metadata, and publishing destination belong to the correct site.

### Gate G — Technical completeness

Validate the output schema, required sections, citations, headings, metadata, and publishing payload.

### Gate H — Publication authorization

Check approval status, site policy, duplicate-publication protection, and publishing permissions.

### Publication rules

A missing required field, invalid publishing payload, severe plagiarism concern, fabricated reference, critical unsupported claim, or cross-site contamination must block publication.

A critical failure cannot be compensated for by a high average quality score.

Use explicit statuses such as:

- `RESEARCH_REQUIRED`
- `DRAFTING`
- `VALIDATION_PENDING`
- `REVISION_REQUIRED`
- `HUMAN_REVIEW_REQUIRED`
- `APPROVED_FOR_DRAFT`
- `APPROVED_FOR_PUBLICATION`
- `PUBLISHED`
- `PUBLICATION_FAILED`
- `QUARANTINED`

Adapt these statuses to the existing application, but preserve the underlying state transitions and safeguards.

## 15. Scoring and Acceptance Criteria

Implement a transparent, configurable quality score. Try to make it better, there is not a hard limit to make it fucking better

Initial proposed weighting:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Factual accuracy and evidence support | 25% |
| Originality and added value | 20% |
| Editorial quality and readability | 20% |
| Research depth and citation integrity | 15% |
| Internal-link correctness | 10% |
| Brief adherence and completeness | 10% |

These weights are starting assumptions and must be calibrated through reviewed test cases.

A provisional acceptance threshold is 90/100, provided that every mandatory gate passes.

The threshold is not proof of quality by itself. The application must retain criterion-level scores, supporting evidence, failures, and reviewer comments.

For critical or high-impact subjects, require stricter review and human approval where appropriate.

A failed article must not enter an endless automatic rewrite loop. Limit retries, preserve previous versions, and escalate unresolved issues.

## 16. Controlled Publishing

- Draft-only mode as the safe default for a newly deployed pipeline.

- Duplicate-publication protection and idempotent requests where supported.
- Recovery from network failures and partial publishing failures.
- A clear distinction between an accepted draft and a successfully published article.
- Version history and rollback or restoration procedures where supported.
- Audit records for every state transition.
- Alerts for blocked jobs and repeated failures.

Never report that an article has been published until the publishing system confirms success.

Do not enable automatic publishing across all websites merely because a small benchmark passes. Enable it only for explicitly approved configurations after the relevant integration has been tested.

## 21. Regression Test: The AOD-9604 Article

Use the supplied failed article as a permanent regression fixture.

The article is titled around AOD-9604 synthetic C-terminal fragment analysis and contains HPLC, mass-spectrometry, stability, calibration, storage, and academic-reference sections.

Known issues to test include:

- PubMed identifiers associated with titles that must be independently verified.
- Conflicting chromatographic conditions and retention times.
- Experimental measurements without traceable underlying data.
- Repeated stability claims across multiple sections.
- Incomplete tables and unclear technical descriptions.
- Image URLs on a domain that may not belong to the intended scientific website.
- Technical recommendations presented without adequate source support.

Required outcome: the regression test must demonstrate that critical citation-integrity and evidence-integrity failures block publication. It must also detect the relevant internal consistency and site-isolation issues.

Do not hard-code the expected conclusion that every scientific measurement is false. The purpose of the test is to verify evidence and flag unsupported or contradictory claims, not to replace one form of unsupported certainty with another.

Keep the fixture private to the appropriate testing context and do not publish it.
